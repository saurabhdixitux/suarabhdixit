How I Stopped a $50k Server From Being Bricked by a Footnote

Enterprise UX is rarely about making things beautiful. This project was about making a system so impossible to misread that a sleep-deprived engineer at 2am couldn't accidentally deploy incompatible firmware on a $50,000 appliance. This is the story of the decisions I made — and the ones that blew up in my face.

The $50,000 Typo

The nightmare wasn't the complexity. It was the spreadsheet.

Riverbed's SteelCentral product line spans a huge range of hardware appliances and software — NetProfiler, AppResponse, NetShark, NetIM, NetAuditor and more. Enterprise deployments almost always combine several of these. And cross-product compatibility isn't just "version A works with version B."

It's more like: NetProfiler 10.19.0 works with AppResponse 9.6.2 — but only if the client has a "Shark Module" license installed. If they don't, the deployment fails silently.

The actual problem I was brought in to solve: Support engineers were managing thousands of these compatibility relationships inside a manually maintained Excel file. Rows and columns of versions. Notes buried in column Z. Caveats like "see KB #29125" sitting between 200 other rows with no visual hierarchy whatsoever.

The actual data structure I inherited. Every caveat lives in Column Z. Engineers miss it constantly.

I sat with three support engineers and watched what happened. One guy had a notepad open on a second monitor where he wrote caveats by hand. Another would ctrl+F through the file and hope he'd searched the right term. The third — who'd been on the team longest — just relied on memory. That's the real failure mode I was designing against.

Embedded Research → Specific Design Decision

I assumed they thought in matrices. I was completely wrong.

Before I drew a single wireframe for the admin interface (the people who enter and manage the data), I ran observation sessions with five database managers. I went in with a hypothesis: these are technical people who live in spreadsheets, so they'll want a grid-style data entry system.

What actually happened: Every single one of them described their mental model using shopping language. "I want to pick a base product, then grab the things that work with it." "It's like adding items to a cart." One person literally mimed dragging objects across a desk.

That one observation session directly changed the entire admin UI architecture. Here's the exact decision it drove:

1
Research finding: Admins think in "Base Product + Things that work with it" — not "Product A ↔ Product B matrix cells."
2
Design decision I made: Abandon the matrix/grid paradigm entirely. Build a "Base Product selector → Pool of targets → Approved bucket" interaction model instead of a spreadsheet-style input.
3
Why this mattered: A grid forces you to think in two dimensions simultaneously. A pool-and-bucket model lets you focus on one product at a time. For a database with 10,000+ nodes, that cognitive load difference is the difference between 4-minute tasks and 45-second tasks.
Research-to-decision trace: one observation session → one architectural choice that shipped
Structure

We had two users with opposite needs. Giving them the same UI was the first mistake we nearly made.

The support engineer checking compatibility needs an answer in under 10 seconds. They're mid-deployment, possibly on a call with a panicking client. They need: search by product, get results, see any caveats immediately — done.

The database admin managing compatibility data needs to see everything at once. They're doing bulk updates during a product release cycle. They need: search the pool, stage entries, attach notes, save batches.

The architectural decision: Hard-split into two separate interfaces sharing the same data layer, rather than one "universal" interface trying to serve both. This felt obvious in hindsight but was a genuine debate — product wanted one tool.

Iteration Archive — The Stuff That Failed

Three designs that died before we shipped.

Recruiters always want to see how you think, not just what you shipped. Here are the iterations that didn't make it, and the specific reasons they were killed.

v0.1 Killed in internal review The Matrix Grid (My First Instinct)
My first wireframe was a classic spreadsheet-style grid. Rows = base products, columns = target products, cells = compatibility status. Visually clean. Logically correct. Completely unusable at scale.

When we stress-tested it with 50 products × 50 versions, the grid had 2,500 cells visible simultaneously. Horizontal scrolling on a laptop screen meant column headers disappeared. Engineers couldn't track which row they were reading. Killed after one usability session.
v0.3 Killed due to data loss risk The Unlocked Dropdown Problem
In v0.3 of the admin interface, we left the "Base Product" selector editable while an admin was mid-session adding entries. The logic: give them flexibility to switch context quickly.

What actually happened in testing: an admin would accidentally click the dropdown while trying to scroll. The base product would change. All their unsaved tags would silently reset. One tester lost 20 minutes of work during a session and didn't notice until she tried to save.

Decision: Lock the base product selector the moment the first tag is added. Show a "You have unsaved changes — switching base product will clear your session" confirmation modal. This single guardrail reduced data loss to zero.
v0.6 Partially shipped — caveats deprioritised Inline Caveats: Too Little Prominence at First
Our first version of the consumer-facing results table showed caveats as a small italic line below the version number. Same visual weight as any other metadata. During testing, three out of four engineers missed the license caveat on the AppResponse row entirely.

The fix: Promote caveats to a distinct amber chip that breaks the normal visual rhythm. Make them impossible to miss by giving them their own row-level visual treatment. This is the shipped version.

Showing Our Losses

The thing I got wrong that I still think about.

We shipped a "Bulk Upload" CSV feature because one admin kept asking for it. I designed it, we built it, and almost no one uses it. The manual tagging flow is fast enough — 45 seconds per mapping — that the CSV workflow only makes sense for bulk release updates, which happen maybe twice a year.

What I should have done differently: Before building the CSV uploader, I should have asked: how often are you doing updates that involve more than 20 entries at once? The answer would have been "almost never." We built for an edge case. The time went to the wrong place.

This is the kind of thing that doesn't show up in a polished case study, but it's exactly the kind of judgment I'm trying to sharpen. Research that reveals what to build is just as valuable as research that reveals what not to build.

"I don't have to keep a notepad open on my second monitor anymore just to remember what I'm mapping."
— Lead Support Engineer, during UAT session

That quote stuck with me. A notepad on a second monitor isn't just an inconvenience — it's a signal that the interface has completely failed as a system. The whole point of a tool is that it holds the context for you. The fact that they'd built workarounds to compensate for the tool's failure was the most honest brief I could have gotten.

The Takeaway

Context isn't a nice-to-have. It's the product.

The entire problem with the old spreadsheet wasn't that the data was wrong. The compatibility information was accurate. The problem was that the system treated exceptions — licenses, patch requirements, KB references — as footnotes. Things you had to go hunting for after you'd already processed the main answer.

The design work here was about promoting those exceptions to first-class citizens in the UI. Every caveat needed to live next to the thing it modified. Every warning needed to be impossible to scroll past without noticing. When you're dealing with a $50,000 server rack and a tired engineer, "might miss it" is not an acceptable failure mode.

If I could go back: I'd spend more time on the caveat-authoring experience for admins. We gave them a free-text note field. What we should have built is a structured caveat editor — license type, KB link, severity — so that the consumer view could surface caveats in a consistent, filterable format rather than just displaying freeform text.
