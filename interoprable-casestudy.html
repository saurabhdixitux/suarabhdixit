01 — The Situation

Every product release made the problem worse. And nobody had a plan.

Riverbed's SteelCentral portfolio is a family of enterprise network monitoring appliances and software — NetProfiler, AppResponse, NetShark, NetIM, NetAuditor. Enterprise clients don't deploy just one. They deploy several, and those products have to talk to each other.

Here's the catch: compatibility isn't binary. It's conditional. NetProfiler 10.19.0 may work with AppResponse 9.6.2 — but only if the client has a "Shark Module" license. Without it, the deployment fails silently.

The team managing these compatibility relationships was doing it inside a manually maintained Excel file. Thousands of rows. Caveats buried in Column Z. Notes like "see KB #29125" sitting between 200 other rows with no visual distinction. Every product release added more rows. Nobody owned the file. The last-updated date was blank.

This is what I inherited on day one. Three support engineers. One spreadsheet. No process.

I watched three engineers work with this file. One had a second notepad open on a second monitor where he wrote caveats by hand before starting a deployment. Another relied on ctrl+F and hoped he'd searched the right term. The third — who'd been there longest — just relied on memory. That was the actual failure mode I was designing against: humans compensating for a tool that had stopped working.

The PM's brief to me was: "customers expect a one-click answer. Internal teams are spending 40% of their time maintaining a file that's already wrong by the time they save it." That was my actual problem statement.

v0.1 matrix grid — killed after one critique session. Works for 5×5. Fails at 50×50.

The specific failure: At 50 products × 50 versions, the grid had 2,500 cells simultaneously. When you scrolled right to see later columns, the row headers disappeared. Engineers couldn't track which product row they were reading. We had rebuilt the same confusion as the spreadsheet, just with better colours.

I scrapped v0.1 after one session and went back to talking to users. The grid wasn't wrong because I made a design mistake. It was wrong because I'd skipped the research step and jumped to a solution. That's the lesson I carry from this project.

04 — The Assumption That Proved Wrong

I assumed they thought in matrices. Every single one of them thought in shopping carts.

Before drawing another wireframe, I ran observation sessions with five database managers — the people who enter and maintain the compatibility data. I went in with a hypothesis: these are deeply technical people who live in spreadsheets. They want a grid-style data entry system.

I was completely wrong.

→
Research Finding
Every single admin described their mental model using shopping language. "I want to pick a base product, then grab the things that work with it." "It's like adding to a cart." One person mimed dragging objects across a desk. Not one person said anything about rows and columns.
∴
Design Decision Made
Abandon the matrix paradigm entirely. Build a "Base Product selector → Pool of targets → Approved bucket" interaction model. One-dimensional. Sequential. One product at a time.
↳
Why This Mattered
A grid forces you to think in two dimensions simultaneously — row context and column context. A pool-and-bucket model lets you focus on one product at a time. At 10,000+ nodes, that cognitive load difference is the difference between 4-minute tasks and 45-second tasks.
Research-to-decision trace: one observation session → one architecture change that shipped

This is also the point where I realised that the problem wasn't just about the admin tool. The support engineers using the consumer side had an equally broken mental model — they were thinking in "can Product A talk to Product B?" but the old system made them think in "find row A, find column B, check cell, now look for notes in column Z". The information architecture was fighting the user's natural workflow.

05 — The Constraints I Couldn't Ignore

I couldn't redesign the backend. I couldn't change the data structure. The sprint was fixed.

Good design decisions always happen inside real constraints. Here were mine — and they directly shaped every tradeoff that followed.

🔒
Backend logic was locked
I couldn't restructure the underlying compatibility database. Data relationships were defined by the engineering team and couldn't change in this sprint.
📦
Legacy products had to remain
The portal had to support end-of-life products that still existed in client deployments — some going back 6+ years.
⏱
4-month delivery window
Fixed. No extension. This meant I had to scope tightly and deprioritise features I believed in — including the caveat-authoring system I'll describe later.
👥
One UI had to serve two user types
Initially. Product wanted a single tool. I had to build a case for a split architecture using data — not just preference.

The constraint that shaped the most decisions: the data structure stayed as-is. This meant I couldn't redesign how relationships were stored — only how they were surfaced and entered. It also meant the "caveats" problem (notes buried in Column Z) could only be solved at the display layer, not at the data layer.

06 — The Hidden Complexity

Two users. Opposite needs. Giving them the same interface would have been a mistake we nearly made.

The support engineer checking compatibility needs an answer in under 10 seconds. They're mid-deployment, probably on a client call. Goal: search → results → caveats → done.

The database admin managing the data needs to see everything simultaneously. They're bulk-updating during a product release cycle. Goal: search pool → stage entries → attach notes → save batches.

The decision product didn't agree with initially: Hard-split into two separate interfaces sharing the same data layer, rather than one "universal" interface serving both. Product wanted one tool. I built the case using the research: one interaction model cannot simultaneously optimise for "answer in 10 seconds" and "manage hundreds of entries at once."

v0.3 Killed — data loss risk The Unlocked Dropdown Problem
We built the pool-and-bucket model but left the Base Product dropdown editable mid-session. The logic: give admins flexibility to switch context quickly without losing their place.

What happened in testing: one admin accidentally clicked the dropdown while trying to scroll past it. Base product changed. All unsaved tags silently reset. She lost 20 minutes of work and didn't notice until she hit save.

Decision: Lock the Base Product selector the moment the first tag is added. Show a modal: "You have unsaved changes — switching will clear your session." This single guardrail reduced data loss incidents to zero.

10 — The Edge Cases That Almost Broke Everything

The problems that only appeared when real data was loaded.

The polished mockup always works. The edge cases are where real systems fail. Here's what we discovered when we loaded actual product data into the prototype.

Edge case 1 — End-of-life products: Some base products had been discontinued but still existed in live client deployments. The original data model had no "EOL" state. In the consumer view, deprecated products appeared identically to current ones. Engineers were selecting EOL products, getting "compatible" results, and deploying firmware that hadn't been supported in two years. We added an EOL badge and a disambiguation message.

Edge case 2 — Bidirectional asymmetry: "NetShark works with AppResponse" does not always mean "AppResponse works with NetShark." Compatibility can be asymmetric — one product initiates a connection type that the other doesn't support in reverse. The first version of the consumer UI didn't communicate direction. We added a "base product perspective" label to every result view.

How we caught them: Both edge cases were discovered in the UAT sessions with Riverbed's own support engineers using real product data — not synthetic test cases. This is why I always push for real data in testing, even when it's harder to set up. Synthetic data hides the mess that real data reveals.

13 — What We Got Wrong

The feature I designed, we built, and almost nobody uses.

One admin kept asking for a bulk CSV upload feature. So I designed it, scoped it, and we built it. The flow was clean: drag a CSV, preview the import, resolve conflicts, save. It looked good in the demo.

Almost nobody uses it. The manual tagging flow is fast enough — 45 seconds per mapping — that the CSV uploader is only useful for major release cycles, which happen twice a year. The other 50 weeks, it sits unused.

What I should have done: Before scoping the CSV uploader, I should have asked one question — "How often are you updating more than 20 entries at once?" The answer would have been "almost never." That question would have revealed the edge case. We built for an exception and treated it like a core feature. The design time belonged elsewhere.

14 — What This Project Taught Me

Four things I know now that I didn't know at the start.

01
Simplicity is almost always an information architecture problem, not a visual design problem. The spreadsheet wasn't hard to read because it was ugly. It was hard to read because caveats and primary data had equal visual weight. That's an IA failure, not a styling failure.
02
Admin UX is harder than customer UX. The consumer view was technically simpler to design. The admin view required me to understand a workflow I had no intuition for, and the consequences of getting it wrong (data loss) were immediate and real. I should have started research there.
03
The first solution is always the wrong solution. I knew this before this project. I still built the grid anyway. The lesson isn't "never trust your first instinct" — it's "validate the instinct before you spend two days building a prototype."
04
One persistent user request is not the same as one validated user need. The CSV uploader came from one person asking loudly. I never tested whether others needed it. That's on me. Volume of requests is not evidence of need.
15 — If I Revisited This Today

Three things I'd change with what I know now.

What I'd do differently
Build the caveat-authoring system first. The biggest limitation of what we shipped is that caveats are free-text strings. An admin types whatever they want, and the consumer view displays it verbatim. What it should be is a structured caveat editor — license type, KB link, severity level — so that the display layer can surface caveats consistently, sort them by severity, and eventually make them filterable. We designed the display first. We should have designed the data entry first.
Model compatibility as a graph, not as card relationships. The current data model is flat — "Product A + Product B + conditions." A graph model would allow the system to infer transitivity: if A works with B and B works with C under condition X, does A work with C? Right now, every relationship has to be entered manually. A graph model would reduce the admin burden by an order of magnitude.
Add a compatibility confidence score. Some relationships in the database are tested quarterly. Others were tested once, two years ago, and never updated. The consumer view treats them all identically. A confidence score — "last validated 8 months ago" vs "validated this release" — would give engineers the context they need to make risk decisions, without adding friction to the happy path.
16 — The Takeaway

Context isn't a nice-to-have. It's the product.

The old spreadsheet wasn't wrong. The compatibility data was accurate. The problem was that the system treated exceptions — licenses, patch requirements, KB references — as footnotes. Things you had to hunt for after you'd already processed the main answer.

The design work here was about promoting those exceptions to first-class citizens in the UI. Every caveat had to live next to the thing it modified. Every warning had to be impossible to scroll past. When you're dealing with a $50,000 appliance and a tired engineer at 2am, "might miss it" is not an acceptable failure mode.

The most important design decision on this project wasn't the dual-pane admin interface or the amber caveat chips. It was the decision to go back and do research after the first prototype failed — instead of iterating on a fundamentally wrong direction. The willingness to throw out two days of work is what made the rest of the project possible.
